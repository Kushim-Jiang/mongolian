The preceding chapters treat one writing system each, but the fonts in common use do not: they cover the whole Mongolian block, and with it Hudum, Todo, Sibe, and Manchu together with their Ali Gali extensions. Such a font is a _single-font implementation_: it integrates several writing systems into one, as [Structure](/architecture/#structure) anticipates. Because the block shares characters between its writing systems, the font must decide which written unit to show for a shared letter at a joining position wherever they disagree. [L2/26-207](https://www.unicode.org/L2/L2026/26207-mongolian.pdf) reviews the disagreements, which fall into three kinds.

**Different letterforms.** The same written unit may be drawn differently: the final form of the letter _m_ (U+182E) has a small tail in Hudum and a large one in Sibe and Manchu. The written unit is the same and only the design differs, so the font keeps each design and selects it with the OpenType `locl` feature under the language system tag of the writing system (MNG, TOD, SIB, MCH).

**Different FVS allocations.** The writing systems do not always agree on which FVS a letter uses. The letter _b_ (U+182A), shared by Hudum, Sibe, and Manchu, selects its final form with FVS1 in Hudum, while for Sibe and Manchu the sequence `U+182A U+180B` is invalid and renders an error form. A writing system and its Ali Gali are declared in one national standard, so a sequence is judged valid by that standard alone: valid in Hudum Ali Gali means valid in Hudum.

**Different default forms.** The writing systems may differ even in the default written unit at a position. U+188A MONGOLIAN LETTER ALI GALI NGA serves both Hudum Ali Gali and Todo Ali Gali: word-finally Hudum Ali Gali has an actual final form, while Todo Ali Gali, having none, renders the final of the Todo letter _ang_ (U+184A). No tag separates them—the Ali Gali writing systems have no language system tags, and tags for Sanskrit or Tibetan, which they transcribe, would not help. A user may therefore meet the actual form of another writing system, which is for that user only a fabricated form derived from another position or writing system—as when a Manchu Ali Gali form appears to users of Sibe and Manchu, who would expect a form derived from the medial.

**Implementing a single font.** The letterform differences are served per writing system, as above; the FVS allocations and the default forms are not, so the font settles them by treating the writing systems as one. At each joining position it shows a single written unit, whatever the writing system of the text. An _actual form_ is one a writing system genuinely writes at the position; a _fabricated form_ is one derived from another position or writing system where the writing system has no form of its own. Consulting the writing systems in the order in which they are integrated—Hudum, Todo, Sibe, Manchu, each Ali Gali after its base—an actual form outranks a fabricated one, and an earlier writing system outranks a later. The unit shown is the actual form of the earliest writing system that has one, or, failing that, the fabricated form of the earliest that supplies one: the font shows the forms that exist and invents none. Whether a variation sequence is valid, and which FVS allocation applies, is decided by the higher-level protocol that shapes the text—the application, or the shaping engine that implements this document—which knows the writing system of the text.

Two characters lie at the edge of this document’s scope, and it specifies no detailed requirements for either:

**[U+1878 MONGOLIAN LETTER CHA WITH TWO DOTS](https://utcstage.unicode.org/Public/18.0.0/charts/nameslist/1800/#1878)**. Used in Buryat Mongolian, proposed by Andrew West, Amgalan Zhamsoev and Viacheslav Zaytsev in [WG2 N4781](https://www.unicode.org/L2/L2017/17007-n4781-mongolian.pdf) and added in Unicode 14.0. Only its medial form is attested in the historical Buryat sources; its initial and final forms were also proposed, as fabricated glyphs.

**[U+1898 MONGOLIAN LETTER TODO ALI GALI TA](https://utcstage.unicode.org/Public/18.0.0/charts/nameslist/1800/#1898)**. Used in no writing system, and perhaps coined by Quejingzhabu. In one scheme the Todo Ali Gali letter _ta_ was analyzed as <Mong TODx links="Dp" />, and the letter _da_ shown the same way, so a stroke was added to tell _da_ apart. All of its glyphs are fabricated.

The first concerns the specifications for Buryat Mongolian and, as [WG2 N4781](https://www.unicode.org/L2/L2017/17007-n4781-mongolian.pdf) indicates, goes beyond this document’s scope; the second appears in no Mongolian writing system, and the current Chinese national standard supports it only for compatibility.

## Reference implementations

The fonts below each serve the whole Mongolian block, so each settles the ambiguity above, and the way it settles it is a reading of this model.

**[Mongolian Baiti](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/typography/font-list/mongolian-baiti)** (`Monbaiti.ttf`). Designed by Founder of Beijing and supplied with Windows since Vista, and with Office beside it, so it is the Mongolian font most users already have. Its script tags declare `Mong` and `Latn`.

**[Noto Sans Mongolian](https://github.com/notofonts/mongolian)**. The Noto project’s Mongolian family, designed by the Monotype design team under the SIL Open Font License, which makes its sources readable alongside this document: the UFO sources, the OpenType Layout features its build composes, and the FontBakery runs that check the result. A specimen and a glyph list are on [Google Fonts](https://fonts.google.com/noto/specimen/Noto+Sans+Mongolian/glyphs). This document’s own reference implementation, [mongfontbuilder](https://pypi.org/project/mongfontbuilder/), was developed alongside it—the test fonts of the [Toolchain](/#toolchain) are its sources—so the two agree by construction on the block’s written units, and their disagreement is where this document is still open to question.

Both are single-font implementations rather than fonts of a single writing system, and the [FVS](/architecture/#format-controls) allocation is the sharpest case: a font of the whole block cannot serve two writing systems that disagree on whether a sequence is valid, so both have had to choose—and this document says how.
