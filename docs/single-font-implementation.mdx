The preceding chapters treat one writing system each, but the fonts in common use do not: they cover the whole Mongolian block, and with it Hudum, Todo, Sibe, and Manchu together with their Ali Gali extensions. Such a font is a _single-font implementation_: it integrates several writing systems into one, as [Structure](/architecture/#structure) anticipates. Because the block shares characters between its writing systems, the font must account for differences in written units and glyph shapes. [L2/26-207](https://www.unicode.org/L2/L2026/26207-mongolian.pdf) reviews these differences. At UTC \#189, the Script Encoding Working Group (SEW) reached consensus that language tags are expected to select language-dependent glyph shapes, including positional variants; when a positional variant does not occur in a language, its rendering is a don't-care, and a form defined for another language may be used. The report requests no action from the UTC ([L2/26-239](https://www.unicode.org/L2/L2026/26239-sew-report-utc189.pdf)).

**Different letterforms.** The same written unit may be drawn differently: the final form of the letter _m_ (<Cp cp="182E" />) has a small tail in Hudum and a large one in Sibe and Manchu. The written unit is the same and only the design differs, so a font can keep each design and select it with the OpenType `locl` feature under the language system tag of the writing system (MNG, TOD, SIB, MCH), in keeping with SEW's guidance.

**Different FVS allocations.** The writing systems do not always agree on which FVS a letter uses. The letter _b_ (<Cp cp="182A" />), shared by Hudum, Sibe, and Manchu, selects its final form with FVS1 in Hudum, while for Sibe and Manchu the sequence `U+182A U+180B` is invalid and renders an error form. A writing system and its Ali Gali are declared in one national standard, so a sequence is judged valid by that standard alone: valid in Hudum Ali Gali means valid in Hudum.

**A position absent from a writing system.** A writing system may have no form of its own at a joining position. For example, <Cp cp="188A" name /> serves both Hudum Ali Gali and Todo Ali Gali: word-finally Hudum Ali Gali has a final form, while Todo Ali Gali has none. Under SEW's guidance, Todo's rendering at that position is a don't-care; a positional form defined for another language may be used. The report does not prescribe a particular fallback for Ali Gali cases where no distinct language tag is available.

**Implementing a single font.** Use language tags to select language-dependent glyph shapes where the text's language is known. If a positional form does not occur in that language, the font is not required to synthesize a particular replacement: the position is a don't-care, and a form used at that position in another language may be supplied instead. This guidance does not establish a universal precedence among writing systems or require one shared written unit at every position. Here, _fabricated form_ continues to describe a form borrowed from another joining position in this document's writing-system data; it is not a requirement that a font use that form as a fallback. Whether a variation sequence is valid, and which FVS allocation applies, remains the responsibility of the higher-level protocol that shapes the text—the application, or the shaping engine implementing this document—which knows the writing system of the text.

Two characters lie at the edge of this document’s scope, and it specifies no detailed requirements for either:

**<Cp cp="1878" name />**. Used in Buryat Mongolian, proposed by Andrew West, Amgalan Zhamsoev and Viacheslav Zaytsev in [WG2 N4781](https://www.unicode.org/L2/L2017/17007-n4781-mongolian.pdf) and added in Unicode 14.0. Only its medial form is attested in the historical Buryat sources; its initial and final forms were also proposed, as fabricated glyphs.

**<Cp cp="1898" name />**. Used in no writing system, and perhaps coined by Quejingzhabu. In one scheme the Todo Ali Gali letter _ta_ was analyzed as <Mong TODx links="Dp" />, and the letter _da_ shown the same way, so a stroke was added to tell _da_ apart. All of its glyphs are fabricated.

The first concerns the specifications for Buryat Mongolian and, as [WG2 N4781](https://www.unicode.org/L2/L2017/17007-n4781-mongolian.pdf) indicates, goes beyond this document’s scope; the second appears in no Mongolian writing system, and the current Chinese national standard supports it only for compatibility.

## Reference implementations

The fonts below each serve the whole Mongolian block, so each settles the ambiguity above, and the way it settles it is a reading of this model.

**[Mongolian Baiti](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/typography/font-list/mongolian-baiti)** (`Monbaiti.ttf`). Designed by Founder of Beijing and supplied with Windows since Vista, and with Office beside it, so it is the Mongolian font most users already have. Its script tags declare `Mong` and `Latn`.

**[Noto Sans Mongolian](https://github.com/notofonts/mongolian)**. The Noto project’s Mongolian family, designed by the Monotype design team under the SIL Open Font License, which makes its sources readable alongside this document: the UFO sources, the OpenType Layout features its build composes, and the FontBakery runs that check the result. A specimen and a glyph list are on [Google Fonts](https://fonts.google.com/noto/specimen/Noto+Sans+Mongolian/glyphs). This document’s own reference implementation, [mongfontbuilder](https://pypi.org/project/mongfontbuilder/), was developed alongside it—the test fonts of the [Toolchain](/#toolchain) are its sources—so the two agree by construction on the block’s written units, and their disagreement is where this document is still open to question.

Both are single-font implementations rather than fonts of a single writing system, and the [FVS](/architecture/#format-controls) allocation is the sharpest case: a font of the whole block cannot serve two writing systems that disagree on whether a sequence is valid, so both have had to choose—and this document says how.
